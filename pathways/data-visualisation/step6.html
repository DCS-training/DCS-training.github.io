Building Graphs

Now that you've got most of the work done, and your data have been cleaned, organised, and prepared, you can finally proceed to create good and reproducible visualisations.

As a researcher, you are often tasked with presenting results, making it important to be able to present the content clearly: visualisations communicate the results of your analysis in a coherent and reproducible way.

If you've done your data wrangling step in either Python or R (or even via OpenRefine), you will have also automatically retained all steps performed (if you have not cancelled the code) and therefore it will be easy to review each stage and also demonstrate your steps if required by the reviewers or publishers.

Principles of Good Data Visualisation

In order to produce good visualisations there are a series of principles to keep in mind:

  • Think about what are you trying to convey and choose the right type of graph accordingly
  • Be accurate and clear e.g. make sure the unit of measurements are clearly expressed
  • Let the data speak: show as much information as possible without hiding any aspects of the data
  • Avoid unnecessary frills and showing off. Less is usually more in visualisations, making them clearer and better
  • Avoid pie charts: they can be very misleading (there are exception to this rule but if you have more than 3-4 categories pie charts are to be avoided because the human eye is not really trained to interpret angles)
  • Consider the medium in which the visualisation will be presented: will the graph be printed or online? how big will it be?

How to choose the right graph?

Choosing the right graph is a really important step in creating clear and informative visualisations:

  • Distribution: Violin, Density, Histogram, Boxplot, Ridgeline
  • Correlation:Scatter, Heatmap, Correlogram, Bubble, Connected Scatter, Density 2d
  • Ranking:Barplot, Spider/Radar, Worldcloud, Lollipop, Circular Barplot
  • Part of a whole:Grouped/Stacked Barplot, Treemap, Doughnut, Pie Chart, Dendrogram, Circular Packing
  • Evolution:Line Plot, Area, Streamchart, Time Series
  • Maps:Choropleth, Hexbin Map, Cartogram, Connection, Bubble Map
  • Flow:Chord Diagram, Network, Arc Diagram, Edge bungling

Building Graphs with R and Python

Two very popular libraries are Matplotlib and Seaborn. Matplotlib is known for the high amount of flexibility it provides as a 2-D plotting library in Python. Seaborn is based on Matplotlib for drawing attractive and informative statistical graphics. Other visualisation libraries are Bokeh, Altair, Plotly, and Ggplot

Data visualisation in R is mainly done via Ggplot. It offers a powerful graphics language for creating elegant and complex plots, with a clearly structured grammar that makes modifying and customising your graphs very easy. This principle is known as Grammar of Graphics. Certain types of graphs will need the use of some additional packages (e.g. in order to create Treemaps you will need to install the Treemap package).
